How to read the root zoneHow to read the root zone

Two items of information have the largest effect on a crop, and you find them when you read the root zone. The first item is if the plant has a water deficit or too much water. The second item is how much water the light causes the plant to use. This guide starts with the facts that you must know before a sensor number gives information. Then it shows three methods to read the water condition: by weight, by probe and by the plant. For each substrate that growers frequently use, it gives a table of start values and a checklist.

Start here

A number gives no information without the facts about the measurement

You cannot know from one number if the plant has a water deficit. Three numbers and the plant show the water condition. You must read them for the substrate that contains the roots.

The two items of information are if the plant has a water deficit or too much water and the water demand that the light causes. You find the two items with the same method: read the root zone. Find the quantity of water in the root zone and the strength of the solution. Also find how quickly the root zone dries, and if the plant agrees with these data. When you read the root zone correctly, the sensors are instruments that you use to control the crop, and not only a dashboard.

The most important information in this guide

A number gives no information if you do not know the facts about the measurement. A moisture value of 40% in coco is not the same as 40% in rockwool. A probe in the incorrect part of a slab does not measure the root zone of your plant. Each method below adds the missing facts to the number.

VWC (volumetric water content)the fraction of the volume of the substrate that is water, in percent. A moisture probe gives this number as its primary indication. The number is only as accurate as the calibration and the position of the probe.
Drybackthe quantity by which the VWC decreases from the peak after one irrigation to the low value before the next irrigation. In each cycle, some dryback is necessary. When the substrate dries, air with oxygen goes into the root zone again. The dryback is also the most important signal. This guide is about the dryback.
Facts first

Know these facts before you find the cause of a problem

Most errors in irrigation are not sensor faults. The error is a reading that you get without the necessary facts. Find these facts one time. Then you can be more sure of each number.

A reading of 42% from a moisture probe is not sufficient information. For a reading of 42%, you must know the substrate, the sensor and the position of the sensor. You must also know the strength of the feed and the quantity of light. If one of these changes, a reading of 42% can show a plant in good condition or a plant with a problem.

Record the facts in the table below before you change a setpoint. Also record the facts before you find that a plant has a water deficit or too much water. Keep this baseline record near the room.

Compare the symptoms

A water deficit and too much water cause the same symptoms

A plant with wilt does not show the cause. Three different faults cause the same wilt. A new grower applies more water. More water corrects one fault, but it can kill the plant when the cause is one of the other two faults.

Wilt occurs when the turgor decreases. The pressure of the water in the cells decreases, and the leaf is no longer rigid. Three causes give the same wilt. You must find the difference between the three causes.

dry
Water deficit

Drought. The substrate has no water that the roots can use. The VWC is low, and the pot is light.

wet
Air deficit

The substrate does not dry, thus there is no oxygen in the pore space. The roots have no oxygen, and rot occurs in the roots. The VWC is high, and it stays high. The wilt is the same as the wilt from drought, thus a new grower applies water again.

EC
Too much salt

Osmotic stress. The substrate has sufficient water, but the salt concentration is very high. The roots cannot absorb the water. The EC is high. The plant cannot get water in a wet pot.

Turgorthe pressure of the water in the cells of the plant. This pressure keeps the leaves and the stems rigid. At lights-on, a plant with full turgor has rigid leaves that point up. A plant without turgor has leaves that bend down. You must find the cause of this symptom.

Use the trend and not one reading to find the cause

A VWC of 45% gives almost no information if you have only this number. A VWC of 45% that decreases while the EC increases shows a substrate that dries and a problem with salt. A VWC of 45% that does not change for six hours while the EC does not change shows a plant with too much water. The number is the same, but the correction is opposite. Thus the shape of the curve gives the information, and not one point.

Know your medium

Information for each substrate

The same shot and the same probe number have different effects in each medium. The media are different in three properties: how they hold water, how they release water and how they become wet again. These properties are the primary information for the difference between a water deficit and too much water. Find the substrate that you use, and use the information in its section.

Two properties are different in each substrate. The first property is how the substrate holds EC. The substrate can have buffering of the feed, or it can give to the roots the same feed that you apply.

The second property is how the substrate becomes wet again after it dries. The next shot can soak the substrate equally, or channeling can occur and the water can flow down the wall and out of the bottom. When you know these two properties for your medium, most faults in irrigation do not start.

CEC (cation exchange capacity)the capacity of the substrate to hold nutrient ions on its surfaces and to give the ions to the roots. The CEC is high in coco and living soil. These substrates have buffering, have a high tolerance for errors, and have a high demand for calcium and magnesium. The CEC is almost zero in rockwool and clay pebbles. These substrates are inert, and you can control them accurately, but they have a low tolerance for errors.
Field capacitythe highest water content that the substrate holds after gravity removes the free water. It is the top of the dryback.
Channelingthe condition when the water flows through a gap (for example down the wall of a pot, or through a dry block) and flows out of the pot as runoff. The water does not wet most of the root zone. This condition gives the most dangerous incorrect signal in cultivation: runoff that shows irrigation, while the root ball has a water deficit.

Rockwool (stone wool)

Rockwool is inert, and its CEC is almost zero. It does not hold nutrients and it has no buffering. Thus the EC in the slab is the EC of the feed that you apply. At saturation, rockwool holds a large quantity of water, but the slope of its water release curve is large. Gravity keeps a saturated layer at the bottom, and the top dries first. This vertical gradient makes the position of the probe in rockwool very important for your data.

The faults of rockwool occur quickly and are easy to see. If rockwool becomes too dry, the EC increases quickly because there is no buffering. Dry stone wool does not become wet again easily. Channeling occurs, and the water does not soak the stone wool. If you apply a large quantity of water quickly to make the rockwool wet again, the bottom has too much water. Then the roots that you dried have no oxygen.

Rockwool checklist
  • Put the probe in the root mass (the top or the middle), and not in the wet bottom. The bottom gives incorrect readings.
  • Know the weight of the slab at saturation and the weight after the dryback. Lift the slab to make sure that the weights are correct.
  • Monitor the pore-water EC while the slab dries. When the pore-water EC is at your EC limit (the highest pore-water EC that you select), the VWC at that time is your working floor.
  • In P1, apply some shots to increase the VWC to field capacity. Do not apply all the water in one large shot.
  • If the slab became too dry, apply some small shots. One large shot causes channeling again.
  • Use more than one probe for each zone. If one probe is different from the other probes, think that its position is incorrect.

Coco coir

Coco has a high tolerance for errors, the same as soil. Its CEC is high, thus coco holds nutrients and releases them to the roots. Coco also gives buffering of the pH, and it decreases the effect of small errors. But coco holds calcium and magnesium on its surfaces, thus a coco crop always has a demand for calcium and magnesium.[5] Water moves through all the profile by capillarity. Thus the water content in a pot in good condition is almost the same from the top to the bottom.

The faults of coco occur more slowly, and it is not easy to see them. If the coco has a water deficit, salt collects because not sufficient solution moves through it. If the coco becomes fully dry, it becomes hydrophobic and channeling occurs. Then the water of the next shot flows down the gap between the root ball and the wall, and out of the bottom. As a result, runoff occurs and you think that irrigation is correct, but the middle of the pot stays dry.

Coco checklist
  • Put the probe in the middle third of the pot, at root depth, approximately one dripper-width from the emitter.
  • Do not let the coco become fully dry. When coco is hydrophobic, channeling occurs, and you cannot see a dry middle.
  • Compare the runoff EC with the feed EC. The result shows if the runoff removes sufficient salt. If the runoff EC increases slowly, the cause is a water deficit.
  • Do the buffering of new coco before you use it. Keep calcium and magnesium in each feed.
  • You can apply larger shots, and less frequently, than in rockwool, because the buffering decreases the effect of errors.
  • After a shot, make sure that the middle of the pot is wet. Use the weight of the pot and not the runoff.

Peat and potting mix (with amendments, or living soil)

Peat and potting mix are a reservoir with a large buffer. They hold a large quantity of water. When they have amendments, or you use them as living soil, they also hold a large quantity of nutrients. Thus the EC readings are not accurate and give less information. The medium has a much higher tolerance for errors in the time of irrigation than an inert slab.

The substrate also has a risk: it is easy to apply too much water because peat dries slowly. Dry peat becomes wet again less easily than the other substrates. It is hydrophobic, thus you must soak it slowly. In some conditions, a wetting agent is also necessary.

In a large pot, the buffer is large, thus strong crop steering does not apply. Apply water. Then let the pot dry to a low weight. Use the weight of the pot to select the time of the next irrigation.

Peat and soil checklist
  • The lift test is your primary reading. Probes give data with much noise in soil with amendments and large pieces.
  • Apply water until a small quantity of runoff occurs. Then let the soil dry to a low water content. Do not keep the soil wet.
  • If the peat became dry, make it wet again slowly. Water that you apply quickly flows off hydrophobic peat.
  • Living soil: do not apply too much feed, and do not change the feed because of the EC. The biology of the soil controls the nutrient supply.
  • Do not let the density of the soil increase. Soil with a high density has not sufficient air for the roots.
  • A larger pot has more buffer, and the cycle is slower. Set the quantity of irrigation for the size of your pot, and not from a table.

Clay pebbles (expanded clay, hydroton, semi-hydroponics)

This substrate changes quickly, and its tolerance for errors is low. Expanded clay drains almost fully. It holds water only as a film, and the reservoir holds the other water. Thus there is almost no dryback buffer, and the substrate must have frequent irrigation or continuous recirculation. The substrate is inert and its CEC is zero. Thus the EC at the roots is the EC of the reservoir, and in a recirculation system this EC changes when the plant uses water.

The substrate is also easy to make wet: it becomes wet again immediately, it does not become hydrophobic, and channeling does not occur. But it has a low tolerance for a dry condition. The primary risk is in the reservoir. If a pump stops, the roots dry in minutes. Rot occurs in roots that are in stagnant water that is warm.

Clay pebble checklist
  • A pump watchdog and a fallback are necessary. This substrate becomes dry quickly when a fault occurs.
  • Monitor the EC, the pH and the temperature of the reservoir, and not the VWC of the substrate.
  • Keep the temperature of the reservoir and of the root zone low. A low temperature keeps the dissolved oxygen high.
  • Use frequent short cycles, because there is no buffer.
  • Keep the drainage clear. Stagnant water at the roots causes rot.
  • Fill the reservoir to a known EC. Do not let the EC increase when the level of the water decreases.
The two faults that the VWC does not show

Do not use only the VWC to find if the root ball is wet. Coco and peat can give incorrect data because channeling or a hydrophobic condition can occur. As a result, runoff does not show that the root ball is wet. Rockwool can give incorrect data because of the gradient. The wet bottom does not show the dry top that has a high EC. The VWC number does not show these two faults, but the weight of the pot and the plant show them.

*The VWC values and the dryback values in these sections are start values, not setpoints. The correct values change with the substrate calibration of your sensor, your cultivar and the type of steering that you want. Set the values when you monitor the dryback and the change of the EC in your room. Do not set a value only because it is in a table.

Read with the probes

The information that the probes give

A substrate probe gives three numbers: the water content, the EC and the temperature. It also gives one shape: the dryback curve. The shape gives more information than each of the numbers.

VWC is the primary number. The first fact to know is that it is possible that the calibration of the VWC is not correct for your substrate. Capacitance probes, for example the TEROS 12, have a calibration for mineral soil when you receive them. In coco or stone wool, this calibration gives readings that are incorrect in the same direction each time. To prevent these incorrect readings, install a calibration for soilless substrates. The GroSens of Grodan has a calibration for stone wool when you receive it, but the TEROS does not.

Thus use the probe for trends and not for the correct value until you calibrate it for your substrate. A probe with a reading that is 8 points too low shows the dryback correctly.

EC is the number that shows osmotic stress before the plant shows it. Table 2 shows this condition. Probes give the bulk EC, but the roots touch the pore-water EC. The pore-water EC is the strength of the solution in the film around the roots, and you calculate it from the bulk EC reading.[9]

The primary property is this: when the substrate dries, the pore-water EC increases. The salts stay when the quantity of water decreases. When you monitor the pore-water EC during the dryback, you find your safe working floor. When the pore-water EC becomes more than your EC limit, the VWC at that time is the working floor. The number of the working floor is not the same each time.

Substrate temperature is the third number, and its effect is not easy to see. A cold slab at lights-on absorbs water slowly. A hot root zone increases the evaporative demand and decreases the oxygen. The substrate temperature also changes the vapor pressure deficit at the leaf. As the section on light shows, the VPD is one of the two items that set the quantity of water that the plant uses.

EC (electrical conductivity)a number that shows the strength of the nutrient solution. It shows how much salt the water contains. When the EC in the substrate increases, the solution becomes stronger, and it is less easy for the roots to absorb water.
Pore-water ECthe EC of the solution that the pores hold. The roots touch this EC. It is different from the bulk EC, which the probe measures. The pore-water EC increases when the substrate dries, also when you add no salt.
The position of the probe is part of the measurement

Put the probe in the middle third of the root zone, at root depth, approximately one dripper-width from the emitter. Do not put the probe in the wet bottom, below the dripper, or at the dry edge. Do not use only one probe. Use more than one probe, and compare each reading with the weight of the pot and with the plant. A probe records the moisture at one point, but the plant uses all the substrate in the pot.

Record the data at a high frequency to see the curve

Record a value at an interval of one to three minutes, and not each hour. The slope of the dryback, the change at each shot, and the change during the night show the cause of a problem. All this information is in the shape. An average for each hour removes the shape and gives a point with no information.

Read with the hands

The lift test and the reference plant

The probe drifts, and its position can give incorrect data. Weight does not have these faults. Thus the lift test is better than the newest sensor for one task: to measure the total quantity of water.

The weight of a pot is its water content. You measure the water content with no calibration and with no gradient, and channeling does not change the result. Know the weight of the pot at saturation and its weight after the dryback.

Then you can find the position of the pot in the dryback when you lift it. The procedure is the lift test. It is not only a method for a new grower. It is the reference for calibration that growers continue to use.

  1. 1
    Find the two reference weights
    Weigh a typical pot immediately after it drains to field capacity. Weigh it again at your selected working floor. Then each time that you lift a pot, you can compare its weight with the two reference weights.
  2. 2
    Put one plant on a scale
    Put a typical plant on a bench scale or a load cell. Record its weight continuously. The plant on the scale is a lysimeter that you can make. The result is a dryback curve with no calibration and no gradient. For the total water, the curve is more accurate than a VWC probe. The weight includes all the root zone at one time, and a probe measures only one point.
  3. 3
    Do a squeeze test of the medium
    Coco: squeeze some coco in your hand. Water that flows out of your hand shows wet coco, and moist coco that stays together is near field capacity. Light coco that breaks into pieces is dry. Rockwool: push the corner of the block, or tilt the slab. Free water shows saturation, and a hard and light block is too dry. A moist block that has the same shape again after you push it is in the working band.
  4. 4
    Read the runoff
    Apply sufficient water for runoff to occur, but not too much water. The volume of the runoff shows how much water flows through the substrate. The most important sign is the runoff EC compared with the feed EC. If the runoff EC is much higher, salt collects because of a water deficit or not sufficient runoff. If the runoff EC is near the feed EC, the salt balance is correct. Monitor how the runoff EC changes each day.
  5. 5
    Do the channeling check
    Immediately after a shot, examine the pot. Find if the pot is heavy and the surface is equally moist. Find also if the water flowed into the tray in seconds while the pot stayed light. Fast runoff and a light pot show that channeling occurred and the root ball is dry. The root ball is dry also if the probe shows a wet substrate.
Lysimetera setup that weighs a plant and its substrate continuously, to measure the quantity of water that flows out of the plant. A lysimeter with one plant on a load cell is a good lysimeter. It is possible that this lysimeter is the most important instrument in the room, and it has no calibration.
Make the load-cell reference plant

If you add one instrument to your probes, put a typical plant on a load cell and record its weight. It gives the correct dryback with no calibration drift and no error from the position. Thus you can find in five seconds if your VWC probe gives incorrect data. When the scale and the probe do not agree about the water, the scale is correct. Find the cause of the error of the probe.

Read with the plant

How to read the plant

The plant shows the total effect of all the conditions that you cannot measure. When you read the plant correctly, it examines your sensors each day, and no equipment is necessary.

Turgor timing is a check that you do each day. A plant with sufficient water has turgor, and its leaves point up at lights-on. A plant with no turgor at the start of the day shows that the water was not sufficient for the demand of the day. A plant that has wilt in the second half of the day, and has turgor again only during the night, shows the same condition. The cause is a water deficit or a P3 dryback that is too deep (the next section gives the definitions of the two causes).

The shape of the leaf shows the difference between the two primary faults. Leaves that bend up, with dry edges and dry tips, can show a water deficit, heat or light stress. Soft leaves that bend down, with no dry edges, can show too much water. The two shapes are different, and you can find the difference after you see the two shapes a small number of times.

Canopy temperature is an instrument with a low cost that gives much information. A plant with a water deficit closes its stomata. As a result, less water flows out of the plant. When the stomata are closed, evaporative cooling stops and the leaf becomes hotter.

Point an infrared thermometer at the canopy and compare the result with a reference plant that has sufficient water. A canopy that is 2 to 3 °C hotter than the reference shows that the stomata close. The standard method is the crop water stress index.[3][4]

But the VPD and the airflow also change the temperature of the leaf. Thus compare the reading with a reference plant. Do not use the reading as the correct value.

CWSI (crop water stress index)a number that shows water stress. You calculate it from the quantity by which the canopy is hotter than a crop with sufficient water in the same air. When the temperature of the canopy minus the temperature of the air increases, the stomata close. As a result, the plant uses less water.
The plant shows the condition and the sensors measure it

When the plant and the probes agree, the reading is correct. When they do not agree, the probe is almost always incorrect. For example, the plant has wilt and the probe shows a wet substrate. The cause is the position of the probe, or the probe does not measure the EC. Use the plant as the reference. Then find the number that is incorrect.

Light and water

How light controls the water demand

Light is the primary control of the water demand. No other item that you control has a larger effect on the water demand. In a room with plants in good condition, the most frequent cause of a water deficit is more light with the same irrigation.

PPFD (photosynthetic photon flux density)the intensity of the light (400–700 nm) that the plant can use, on the canopy, in µmol/m²/s. It is the intensity of the light at this time.
DLI (daily light integral)the total of the light that the plant can use during all the photoperiod, in mol/m²/day. DLI = PPFD × photoperiod-hours × 0.0036. It shows the total light of the day in one number. The water demand of the plant changes with the DLI at almost the same rate.

The physiology is a short sequence of steps. Photosynthesis is possible only when the stomata are open, because CO₂ goes into the leaf through the open stomata. Open stomata also release water vapor. Transpiration is this flow of water vapor out of the leaf, and it pulls water up from the roots. When the light increases, there is more photosynthesis, more open stomata, and more water in the plant.

But the light sets only the demand. The vapor pressure deficit (how much more moisture the air can hold) sets the rate at which water flows out through the open stomata. The quantity of water that the plant uses is approximately the product of the two: the conductance that the light causes multiplied by the VPD.[1][2] If you increase the light or the VPD, the plant uses more water. If you increase the light and the VPD, the plant uses much more water.

VPD (vapor pressure deficit)the quantity of moisture that the air can absorb before it saturates. It is the drying power of the air. A high VPD pulls water out of the open stomata more quickly. The VPD multiplies the demand that the light causes.

In the usual range of operation, transpiration changes with the light and the DLI almost in a straight line. The yield of cannabis also changes with the light intensity almost in a straight line.[7][8] The straight line continues up to very high PPFD, and up to a higher PPFD with CO₂ enrichment. Two effects change this straight line.

Above the light saturation point of a cultivar, more photons add only a small quantity of photosynthesis, but they add heat from radiation. As a result, the plant continues to use more water, but the growth does not increase, and the efficiency decreases.

CO₂ enrichment closes the stomata, but not fully. Thus each leaf transpires less for each unit of light (a better water-use efficiency). But a room with CO₂ enrichment has a larger crop with more light and heat. Thus the total quantity of water that the room uses usually increases and does not decrease. Each leaf uses less water, but the total quantity of water is larger.

The light also sets when the plant uses water, because the transpiration changes with the light curve during the day. The phases of crop steering use this fact.

  1. P0: dryback during the night. There is no irrigation, and the plant uses the water in the pot when the lights are off. This phase sets the quantity of air in the roots and the quantity of generative steering at the start of the day.
  2. P1: ramp and saturation. The shots occur after lights-on. They increase the water content of the pot again to field capacity when the demand increases.
  3. P2: maintenance. The shots occur during the day. They keep the VWC (or change it slowly for steering) through the peak transpiration.
  4. P3: last shot. The shot in this phase is the last irrigation before lights-off. Its time sets the depth of the dryback during the night.

A water deficit that the grower causes

When you increase the light, increase the water before the plant shows a symptom. A grower increases the PPFD (for example from 600 to 900) for more yield, but keeps the same irrigation. The water demand increases by approximately 40 to 50%. The pot dries to less than its working floor.

The EC increases during the dryback, and it increases very quickly in rockwool. The plant has osmotic stress, and wilt occurs while the lights are on. The symptoms are the same as heat stress or light stress. Thus the grower decreases the light and does not add water. As a result, the grower does not get the larger yield that the higher light must give.

All the parts together

The loop of tasks for each day

All the information above gives one loop of tasks for each day. Set the quantity of water from the light. Set the rate from the air. Limit the dryback with the EC. Use the weight and the plant to make sure that the three tasks are correct.

  1. 1
    Quantity of water from the DLI
    The light of the day sets how much water the crop uses. When you go to a higher value in Table 3, increase the quantity of water that you supply each day. This first adjustment is large.
  2. 2
    Rate from the VPD and the light curve
    Set the shots for the shape of the day. Increase the shots in P1. Keep the water content stable in P2 through the peak at the middle of the day. Decrease the shots in P3. The VPD sets how quickly the demand increases.
  3. 3
    Working floor from the EC limit
    Select the depth of the dryback. The pore-water EC limits the depth. When the EC is at your EC limit, the VWC at that time is your working floor. The working floor is the limit between steering and a water deficit.
  4. 4
    Examine the weight and the plant
    Compare the probe with the lift test or with the load-cell plant. Also compare it with the turgor and the canopy temperature. If the results do not agree, examine the cause. The cause is frequently the position, channeling or the EC.
  5. 5
    Adjust the dryback for steering
    A deeper P0 dryback and a higher EC limit give generative steering. A smaller dryback and a lower EC give vegetative steering. You select the position of the working floor, and you do not find it by accident.
The same VWC line can show steering or a water deficit

A controlled dryback with a known EC limit, and a plant that has turgor again during the night, is generative steering. In this condition, you selected the working floor and you keep to it. The same VWC line can continue after the pore-water EC is at the EC limit. If the plant has wilt and does not have turgor again, this line is a water deficit. In this condition, you went to a VWC that is less than the working floor by accident. The probe number can be the same, thus always know if you have steering or a water deficit.

Procedures

Short instructions for good results

The list below is a summary. It shows the difference between a person who has sensors and a person who reads them correctly.

  • The trend is more important than the value. A probe without calibration shows the dryback correctly. Calibrate the probe to get the correct number, and use the shape from the first day.
  • Use a weight measurement and not an estimate. A load-cell reference plant is the most important instrument that you can add, and it has no drift. When the probe and the scale do not agree about the water, the scale is correct.
  • The position of the probe is part of the measurement. Use the middle third, root depth, and one dripper-width from the emitter. The wet bottom and the dry edge give incorrect data in opposite directions.
  • Let the EC set the working floor. The pore-water EC that increases during the dryback is your limit. When it is at your EC limit, stop the dryback. The VWC at that time is the working floor, and its number is not the same each time.
  • Know how your substrate becomes wet again. Channeling occurs in coco that is too dry, dry peat is hydrophobic, and the bottom of rockwool has too much water. Clay pebbles have no problem. Do not let coco, peat or rockwool become fully dry. If they do, apply some small shots, and not one large shot.
  • Compare the runoff EC with the feed EC. The result shows how much salt the runoff removes. If the runoff EC increases slowly while the feed EC is stable, there is a water deficit. Increase the runoff percentage before the salt that collects in the substrate causes damage.
  • Do a check of the turgor in the morning and of wilt in the afternoon. Do this check each day, and no equipment is necessary. A plant with no turgor at lights-on shows that the water of the day was not sufficient for the light of the day. A plant with wilt that stops only during the night shows the same condition.
  • Keep an infrared thermometer on the bench. A canopy that is hotter than a reference plant with sufficient water shows that the stomata close. The plant has stress. Make sure that the result is correct before you apply a correction.
  • Use P3 for steering and P0 for air in the roots. The time of the last shot sets the dryback during the night. The dryback during the night sets the oxygen in the roots and the quantity of generative or vegetative steering.
  • Monitor the substrate temperature at lights-on. A cold slab absorbs water slowly. Increase the shots in the morning in steps. Do not apply a large quantity of cold feed to a cold medium.
  • Change the water before you change the light. When the DLI increases, the water demand changes immediately. Increase the water that you supply first. Thus the plant does not have to show wilt as the first sign.
  • Do not compare a number from a Grodan probe with a number from a TEROS probe. The scales are not the same, because the substrates and the calibrations are different. Compare each probe only with the previous data of the same probe.
  • One probe gives data for only one point. Use more than one probe for each zone. If one probe is different from the other probes, think that its position is incorrect until you find a different cause.
  • A water problem frequently causes the symptoms that make you decrease the light or the temperature. Examine the VWC, the EC and the weight before you change the environment.

The summary on one screen

Four checks show if the plant has a water deficit or too much water, and they give the result more quickly than a dashboard:

WeightLift the pot, or read the scale. A pot that is light and becomes lighter quickly has a water deficit. A pot that is heavy and stays heavy has too much water.
DrybackA deep and fast dryback, with the VWC less than the working floor, shows a water deficit. A flat line shows too much water, because air does not go into the root zone again.
Change of the ECAn EC that increases shows that the salt concentration increases: there is a water deficit or not sufficient runoff. An EC that decreases shows that the solution has more water: there is too much water.
PlantA plant with no turgor at lights-on, a hot canopy, and leaves that bend up shows a water deficit. Soft leaves that bend down, gnats and algae show too much water.

The summary of this guide

The water condition is weight + dryback + EC + the plant, and you read these items for the medium. Each substrate holds EC and becomes wet again with different properties. DLI × VPD sets the water demand. Thus each time that you increase the light, you also increase the water demand.

The numbers in this guide

Each VWC value, EC value and dryback value in this guide is a start value for your decisions. It is not a setpoint for your room. Your calibration, substrate, genetics and type of steering change all of them. Set the values when you monitor how your root zone changes. The method is the same for all rooms, but each room has different numbers.

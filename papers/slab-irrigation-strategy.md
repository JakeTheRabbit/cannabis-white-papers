# Rockwool Slab Irrigation

_Water and substrate · ~18 min to read_

> Rockwool blocks and slabs: volume of the substrate, flow rate of the drippers, drainage, root establishment, P0–P3 irrigation phases and root-zone EC.

## Purpose and scope

Use the measured flow rate of the drippers to calculate the quantity of water to supply. Make sure that the water goes into the block and that the roots go into the slab. Make sure that the runoff flows out of the tray. After these checks, use the trends of the VWC and the EC to change the irrigation.

In vegetative steering, you fill the substrate again more frequently and the dryback is smaller. In generative steering, the irrigation window is shorter and the dryback is larger. Athena and CCI give information on how to change these conditions during the growth of the crop. The ranges of numbers below are examples from their documentation.[[13]](#ref-13)[[16]](#ref-16)

Changes to irrigationFor a usual adjustment, change one irrigation control and record the effect during one light cycle. Immediately correct a dripper with a blockage, a shot that did not occur, and sudden water stress. Record the correction and examine the plant for a change.

## Definitions

Field capacityThe water content that stays in a saturated substrate after the free water drains out, in specified conditions. In a slab, the measured value changes if the properties of the substrate are different. It also changes if the shape and size of the slab, the drainage openings or the position of the sensor are different.Absolute drybackThe quantity by which the volumetric water content (VWC) decreases from the peak to the trough, in percentage points. When the VWC decreases from 70% to 50%, the dryback is 20 points.Relative drybackThe absolute dryback divided by the start VWC, multiplied by 100. When the VWC decreases from 70% to 42%, the absolute dryback is 28 percentage points. The relative dryback is 40% of the start value.P0 / P1 / P2 / P3P0 is the time from lights-on to the first irrigation. P1 is the phase that fills the substrate again. P2 is the phase with the maintenance irrigations. P3 is the time from the last irrigation to the next lights-on. The total dryback between the irrigation windows includes P3 and the P0 after it. The terms are not the same in all controllers.Root-zone ECYou measure the feed EC and the runoff EC in the solution. You measure the bulk EC through the substrate. You can calculate an estimate of the pore-water EC from the data of the sensor. Compare readings of the same measurement method at almost the same VWC. The unit is mS/cm. The number is the same as the number in dS/m.[[18]](#ref-18)RunoffThe drain volume divided by the applied volume, as a percentage. Catch the runoff from some typical plants or slabs in each irrigation zone. Do not use the pump time to calculate it.

When the VWC decreases from 70% to 50%, the absolute dryback is 20 percentage points. The relative dryback is approximately 28.6% of the start water content. These values are only an example. ## Movement of water between the block and the slab

The block above a wet slab can stay dry. Examine the block, and not only the slab, during root establishment.

Water moves through connected rockwool along hydraulic gradients. After drainage, the top areas can keep less water. The properties of the substrate, the irrigation and the uptake of water by the roots cause the water distribution.[[5]](#ref-5)[[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7)

When the fibers of the block and the fibers of the slab touch fully, water can move between the block and the slab. The staged-drainage method of Grodan gives information on the drying of the block that the slab causes. It also shows that, if the slab keeps water at the start, this effect can be smaller. The drainage opening changes the quantity of water that the slab keeps and the drainage of the block.[[3]](#ref-3)

Water moves between the block and the slab through their contact face. When the media drain, the top media can become drier and the slab can stay wet. The irrigation and the uptake also change the profile. The profile is different for different irrigation and drainage conditions. In its staged-drainage method, Grodan shows that water that the slab keeps changes the drying of the block. [[3]](#ref-3) Move the diagram to the side to see the labels at full size.

## Slab saturation and drainage openings

Saturate the slabs equally in all parts. Make the drainage openings. Put each block directly on the fiber of the slab, with no plastic between the block and the fiber.

1. 1Examine the surface below the slabBefore you put the slabs on the tray, examine the tray and the direction in which the runoff flows. Make sure that the surface below each slab is the same for all slabs. Let the runoff flow to the runoff container.
2. 2Saturate the slab with the nutrient solutionSaturate the slab with the nutrient solution through the specified openings. Record the EC and the pH. The staged-drainage method of Grodan gives an instruction: keep the slab saturated for a minimum of 24 hours.[[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3)
3. 3Select the drainage procedureIn the staged-drainage method of Grodan, you first open the wrapper above the seal. Then only a part of the water drains. After this, you make the openings below the first opening. Obey the procedure of the product that you select for the position and the time of each opening.[[3]](#ref-3)
4. 4Open the wrapper at the position of each blockObey the instructions on the wrapper. Then each block goes on the fiber of the slab, with no plastic below the contact face of the block.[[2]](#ref-2)
5. 5Put the blocks on the slab and examine themPut each block, with the roots of its plant in the block, on the slab. Make sure that the fibers of the block and the slab touch on a flat face. Examine the water content of the blocks and the roots. Do this before you use the procedure for root establishment that you select.

## Irrigation after transplanting

At the start, the roots are in the block of the transplant. The irrigation must keep this block wet while the roots become longer and go into the slab.

The volume of 7.35 L for each plant, which this paper uses to calculate the shot time, includes slab volume. It is possible that the new roots are not in this volume at this time. Set the irrigation at the start from the water content of the block and the growth of the roots.

1. 1Record the condition at the transplantAt the transplant, record the water content of the block and the VWC of the slab. Also record the feed EC, how the block touches the slab, and the roots that you can see.
2. 2Examine the water content of the blockMake sure that the water goes into the block and makes it wet equally in all parts. Examine the block between the shots and after the irrigation.
3. 3Examine the growth of the rootsExamine the roots at the interface of the block and the slab. Record if the water uptake increases when the roots go into the slab.
4. 4Do a check of root establishmentUse the cycle of each day for plants after root establishment. Use it when the roots are in the slab and the measured uptake is stable. Before you increase the irrigation, make sure that the water supply and the drainage are correct.

## Climate and water uptake

Light, temperature, humidity and air movement have an effect on the transpiration. After a change of the climate or the lighting, compare three values with the values before the change. The three values are the rate at which the VWC decreases, the volume of irrigation and the runoff. Measure at the near end, the middle and the far end of the table. If one area dries more quickly, examine the local climate and the supply from the drippers. Do this before you make a change for all of the zone.

#### Plant clearance and lollipopping at the start

In this layout, the plants must be sufficiently high to keep the foliage and branches clear of the bars and their supports. At the start, use lollipopping to remove the bottom growth that can touch the fixtures. Also remove the bottom growth that touches the air socks or fills the space below the canopy. Keep the foliage that makes the crop above the lights. When the branches become wider and the flowers become heavier, examine the clearance again. Use the minimum crop clearance of the fixture manufacturer.

Foliage that touches a fixture, or that is very near to a fixture, can have local damage from heat or light. Bottom growth with a high density also causes a blockage of the airflow, and it is not easy to examine the plants. If the plants are too short for the bars, do not use the bars until the plants are higher. You can also put the bars in a different position.

#### How to move light from the top to the bottom of the canopy

You can use under-canopy lighting to move part of the lighting power from the top of the canopy to the bottom. Decrease the setting of the top lights in steps. At the same time, measure the light through the crop. The same electrical watts give the same total lighting power. But the efficacy, the spectrum and the light interception are not the same for all fixtures. As a result, the same watts do not always give the same photon output or the same effect on the crop.

You measure PPFD at one surface and in one direction. It is not correct to subtract a reading below the canopy from a reading above it. Measure the PPFD of the top, middle and bottom foliage, with the same sensor positions and directions each time. Also include the photoperiod in the results. The tests in cannabis add under-canopy light to top light. These tests do not show that the same quantity of bottom light always replaces top light.[[20]](#ref-20)

#### Heat and root-zone temperature

When you add lighting power, the heat that the room must remove increases. If you decrease the watts of the top lights by the same quantity, the total lighting power stays almost the same. But the heat is then near the slabs. The air socks move the heat to other areas, but the cooling system or the exhaust must remove it. Keep the fixtures away from the wrappers of the slabs and let air flow around the bars. Make sure that the cooling surfaces of the bars are clear.

Record the temperature in typical slabs with roots. Include positions near the bars and positions with a lower temperature to compare. Also record the air temperature and the leaf temperature in the bottom canopy. A room sensor above the crop cannot always find an area with a high temperature below it. Compare the temperature trend for the full lights-on period before and after each change of the lighting. Also compare the VWC, the EC and the runoff.

When the root zone is too hot, the roots and the microbes use more oxygen, and warm water holds less dissolved oxygen. In rockwool, the pores that contain air and the drainage also supply the oxygen. Thus a slab that is warm and stays wet for a long time can give the roots oxygen that is not sufficient. The uptake of water and nutrients can decrease. As a result, the plant can show wilt although the substrate is wet, and the growth can be unsatisfactory and the yield can be lower. Warm conditions can help the growth of some species of Pythium, but only when the pathogen is in the root zone.[[21]](#ref-21)

If the slab temperature is more than the limit for the crop, stop the under-canopy bars or decrease their light. Do the same if the plants show damage after the change of the lighting. Examine the cooling, the clearance, the airflow and the drainage. Examine the roots and make sure that the condition of the plants becomes good again. More irrigation without a check of the cause can decrease the oxygen in a slab that is wet.

Start here

Purpose and scope

Use the measured flow rate of the drippers to calculate the quantity of water to supply. Make sure that the water goes into the block and that the roots go into the slab. Make sure that the runoff flows out of the tray. After these checks, use the trends of the VWC and the EC to change the irrigation.

In vegetative steering, you fill the substrate again more frequently and the dryback is smaller. In generative steering, the irrigation window is shorter and the dryback is larger. Athena and CCI give information on how to change these conditions during the growth of the crop. The ranges of numbers below are examples from their documentation.[13][16]

Changes to irrigation

For a usual adjustment, change one irrigation control and record the effect during one light cycle. Immediately correct a dripper with a blockage, a shot that did not occur, and sudden water stress. Record the correction and examine the plant for a change.

Terms

Definitions

Field capacityThe water content that stays in a saturated substrate after the free water drains out, in specified conditions. In a slab, the measured value changes if the properties of the substrate are different. It also changes if the shape and size of the slab, the drainage openings or the position of the sensor are different.
Absolute drybackThe quantity by which the volumetric water content (VWC) decreases from the peak to the trough, in percentage points. When the VWC decreases from 70% to 50%, the dryback is 20 points.
Relative drybackThe absolute dryback divided by the start VWC, multiplied by 100. When the VWC decreases from 70% to 42%, the absolute dryback is 28 percentage points. The relative dryback is 40% of the start value.
P0 / P1 / P2 / P3P0 is the time from lights-on to the first irrigation. P1 is the phase that fills the substrate again. P2 is the phase with the maintenance irrigations. P3 is the time from the last irrigation to the next lights-on. The total dryback between the irrigation windows includes P3 and the P0 after it. The terms are not the same in all controllers.
Root-zone ECYou measure the feed EC and the runoff EC in the solution. You measure the bulk EC through the substrate. You can calculate an estimate of the pore-water EC from the data of the sensor. Compare readings of the same measurement method at almost the same VWC. The unit is mS/cm. The number is the same as the number in dS/m.[18]
RunoffThe drain volume divided by the applied volume, as a percentage. Catch the runoff from some typical plants or slabs in each irrigation zone. Do not use the pump time to calculate it.
Equipment

Slab layout and flow rate of the drippers

Shot time = necessary volume ÷ measured flow rate. Each table is 7.6 × 1.2 m and has three rows of seven slabs. Each slab has three plants. Thus each table has 63 plants and seven lights above the plants, with nine plants for each light. Three tables have 189 plants.

SubstrateRockwool blocks of 15 cm (6 in), approximately 3.6 L (0.95 gal), on slabs of 1 m (39 in)
Volume of the root zone for each plantApproximately 7.35 L (1.9 gal) for each plant. The root zone of each plant is a block of 3.6 L (0.95 gal) plus one third of a slab of 11.25 L (3.0 gal). The volume of the slab is a value that this paper uses to calculate the root zone.
Water supply to the blockPressure-compensating drippers, short feed tubes, and a ring on the top of the block. You can use a different distributor that makes a wide wet area, if you do a catch test of it.
Dimensions of the table and number of plants

After you put seven slabs of 1 m on the table, a space of 300 mm stays at each end. Two air socks go between the three rows of slabs. A 200 mm fan supplies each air sock, and one fan supplies only one air sock.

The under-canopy LEDs go in one of two positions. They go along the table above the two air socks, or across the table at equal intervals. Install the fixtures above the fabric. Do not attach the supports of the fixtures to the fabric.

The width of the table is 1.2 m. Three rows of slabs of 150 mm and two inflated air socks use 856 mm of the width. A width of 344 mm stays for gaps and hardware. This paper uses 203 mm for the diameter of an inflated air sock. Measure the diameters of the inflated air socks, the clearances of the fixtures and the airflow.

Dripper layouts and shot time

A shot is one irrigation event. This paper uses a volume of 7.35 L for each plant to calculate the shot volume. With this volume, 3% is 220.5 mL and 5% is 367.5 mL. The table uses the specified flow rate of the drippers. Replace it with the volume that you collect, divided by the time in which you collect it.

Variation of the flow rate of the outlets

Do a catch test of the outlets that are near the manifold and far from the manifold. Include the outlets at the start and at the end of the longest active zone. Examine the filters. Follow the specification of the dripper manufacturer when you flush the laterals. If one plant is dry and the plants near it have the usual water content, measure the outlet flow rate. Then examine how the block touches the slab, and the condition of the roots.

Water movement

Movement of water between the block and the slab

The block above a wet slab can stay dry. Examine the block, and not only the slab, during root establishment.

Water moves through connected rockwool along hydraulic gradients. After drainage, the top areas can keep less water. The properties of the substrate, the irrigation and the uptake of water by the roots cause the water distribution.[5][6][7]

When the fibers of the block and the fibers of the slab touch fully, water can move between the block and the slab. The staged-drainage method of Grodan gives information on the drying of the block that the slab causes. It also shows that, if the slab keeps water at the start, this effect can be smaller. The drainage opening changes the quantity of water that the slab keeps and the drainage of the block.[3]

If the block becomes dry

If the block becomes dry, measure the outlet flow rate. Examine how the fibers touch and the condition of the roots. When you make the block wet again, make sure that the water goes into the block. Make sure that it does not flow around the block. After this, examine the water content of the block and the condition of the plant.

Prepare the slabs

Slab saturation and drainage openings

Saturate the slabs equally in all parts. Make the drainage openings. Put each block directly on the fiber of the slab, with no plastic between the block and the fiber.

  1. 1
    Examine the surface below the slab
    Before you put the slabs on the tray, examine the tray and the direction in which the runoff flows. Make sure that the surface below each slab is the same for all slabs. Let the runoff flow to the runoff container.
  2. 2
    Saturate the slab with the nutrient solution
    Saturate the slab with the nutrient solution through the specified openings. Record the EC and the pH. The staged-drainage method of Grodan gives an instruction: keep the slab saturated for a minimum of 24 hours.[2][3]
  3. 3
    Select the drainage procedure
    In the staged-drainage method of Grodan, you first open the wrapper above the seal. Then only a part of the water drains. After this, you make the openings below the first opening. Obey the procedure of the product that you select for the position and the time of each opening.[3]
  4. 4
    Open the wrapper at the position of each block
    Obey the instructions on the wrapper. Then each block goes on the fiber of the slab, with no plastic below the contact face of the block.[2]
  5. 5
    Put the blocks on the slab and examine them
    Put each block, with the roots of its plant in the block, on the slab. Make sure that the fibers of the block and the slab touch on a flat face. Examine the water content of the blocks and the roots. Do this before you use the procedure for root establishment that you select.
Root establishment

Irrigation after transplanting

At the start, the roots are in the block of the transplant. The irrigation must keep this block wet while the roots become longer and go into the slab.

The volume of 7.35 L for each plant, which this paper uses to calculate the shot time, includes slab volume. It is possible that the new roots are not in this volume at this time. Set the irrigation at the start from the water content of the block and the growth of the roots.

  1. 1
    Record the condition at the transplant
    At the transplant, record the water content of the block and the VWC of the slab. Also record the feed EC, how the block touches the slab, and the roots that you can see.
  2. 2
    Examine the water content of the block
    Make sure that the water goes into the block and makes it wet equally in all parts. Examine the block between the shots and after the irrigation.
  3. 3
    Examine the growth of the roots
    Examine the roots at the interface of the block and the slab. Record if the water uptake increases when the roots go into the slab.
  4. 4
    Do a check of root establishment
    Use the cycle of each day for plants after root establishment. Use it when the roots are in the slab and the measured uptake is stable. Before you increase the irrigation, make sure that the water supply and the drainage are correct.

Measurements of a slab with three plants

In episode 31 of We The Growers, Sipkoi tells how to collect the irrigation water and the runoff from one slab with its three plants. When you divide the quantities by three, the result is the average for each plant. The average cannot show which dripper has a blockage.[15]

Procedure from the podcast, for that facility only

After approximately 16 days in the vegetative stage, the plants go from blocks of 10 × 10 × 6.4 cm (4 × 4 × 2.5 in) to slabs with saturated substrate. One slab is above a runoff tray. Personnel divide the applied volume and the drained volume by the three plants of the slab. They record the volume in and the volume out each day. They measure the pH and the EC of the runoff one or two times each week.

In the first 48 hours on the slabs, the start procedure in the podcast is approximately 12 shots during the lights-on period. Each shot is two minutes through an outlet of 1.9 L/h (0.5 GPH). Then the grower stops the irrigation for a period of three to four days. After this, the grower increases the irrigation to the full quantity. At approximately day 14, a typical procedure in the podcast is 10 to 12 shots of four minutes. The grower changes the frequency for the quantity of water that the cultivar uses and for the drainage that the grower sees.

The podcast gives information on how to put the slabs in position at 27:33-28:12. It gives information on how to collect the runoff from a slab at 31:43-32:33. Control of nutrients

Root-zone EC and nutrition before harvest

Before you correct a high EC, examine the measurement method and the water supply. Do the product procedures before harvest and the diagnosis of salt that collects in the substrate as two different tasks.

  1. 1
    Examine the EC measurement
    Find the type of the EC. It can be the feed EC, the runoff EC, the bulk EC, or an estimate of the pore-water EC. Examine the calibration. Make sure that the probe touches the substrate correctly. Compare the readings of the substrate at almost the same VWC.[18]
  2. 2
    Salt that collects in the substrate
    If readings that you can compare continue to increase, measure the feed EC. Also measure the volume that the system supplies and the drainage that you collect. Drainage removes dissolved salts. After you change the feed or the irrigation, examine the next readings of the runoff and the substrate.
  3. 3
    Athena Fade procedure
    In the nine-week example of Athena, Fade replaces Pro Core in weeks 8 and 9, and Pro Bloom continues. The example gives these instructions: keep the feed EC, and measure the pH after Fade replaces Pro Core. This example is a procedure for these products. The example does not show that this procedure is necessary for all cannabis in rockwool.[17]
  4. 4
    Last irrigation step of Athena
    On the last day in rockwool, the same example uses Cleanse in water from reverse osmosis. The example also uses measurements of the EC to see the result. This procedure does not show if the quality of the flower becomes better when you flush the substrate before harvest.[17]
Environment

Climate and water uptake

Light, temperature, humidity and air movement have an effect on the transpiration. After a change of the climate or the lighting, compare three values with the values before the change. The three values are the rate at which the VWC decreases, the volume of irrigation and the runoff. Measure at the near end, the middle and the far end of the table. If one area dries more quickly, examine the local climate and the supply from the drippers. Do this before you make a change for all of the zone.

Plant clearance and lollipopping at the start

In this layout, the plants must be sufficiently high to keep the foliage and branches clear of the bars and their supports. At the start, use lollipopping to remove the bottom growth that can touch the fixtures. Also remove the bottom growth that touches the air socks or fills the space below the canopy. Keep the foliage that makes the crop above the lights. When the branches become wider and the flowers become heavier, examine the clearance again. Use the minimum crop clearance of the fixture manufacturer.

Foliage that touches a fixture, or that is very near to a fixture, can have local damage from heat or light. Bottom growth with a high density also causes a blockage of the airflow, and it is not easy to examine the plants. If the plants are too short for the bars, do not use the bars until the plants are higher. You can also put the bars in a different position.

How to move light from the top to the bottom of the canopy

You can use under-canopy lighting to move part of the lighting power from the top of the canopy to the bottom. Decrease the setting of the top lights in steps. At the same time, measure the light through the crop. The same electrical watts give the same total lighting power. But the efficacy, the spectrum and the light interception are not the same for all fixtures. As a result, the same watts do not always give the same photon output or the same effect on the crop.

You measure PPFD at one surface and in one direction. It is not correct to subtract a reading below the canopy from a reading above it. Measure the PPFD of the top, middle and bottom foliage, with the same sensor positions and directions each time. Also include the photoperiod in the results. The tests in cannabis add under-canopy light to top light. These tests do not show that the same quantity of bottom light always replaces top light.[20]

Heat and root-zone temperature

When you add lighting power, the heat that the room must remove increases. If you decrease the watts of the top lights by the same quantity, the total lighting power stays almost the same. But the heat is then near the slabs. The air socks move the heat to other areas, but the cooling system or the exhaust must remove it. Keep the fixtures away from the wrappers of the slabs and let air flow around the bars. Make sure that the cooling surfaces of the bars are clear.

Record the temperature in typical slabs with roots. Include positions near the bars and positions with a lower temperature to compare. Also record the air temperature and the leaf temperature in the bottom canopy. A room sensor above the crop cannot always find an area with a high temperature below it. Compare the temperature trend for the full lights-on period before and after each change of the lighting. Also compare the VWC, the EC and the runoff.

When the root zone is too hot, the roots and the microbes use more oxygen, and warm water holds less dissolved oxygen. In rockwool, the pores that contain air and the drainage also supply the oxygen. Thus a slab that is warm and stays wet for a long time can give the roots oxygen that is not sufficient. The uptake of water and nutrients can decrease. As a result, the plant can show wilt although the substrate is wet, and the growth can be unsatisfactory and the yield can be lower. Warm conditions can help the growth of some species of Pythium, but only when the pathogen is in the root zone.[21]

If the slab temperature is more than the limit for the crop, stop the under-canopy bars or decrease their light. Do the same if the plants show damage after the change of the lighting. Examine the cooling, the clearance, the airflow and the drainage. Examine the roots and make sure that the condition of the plants becomes good again. More irrigation without a check of the cause can decrease the oxygen in a slab that is wet.
